Cloud sits on Cradle Mountain like a lid. Down at Dove Lake the water is the colour of strong tea, the wind smells of wet button grass and cold stone, and every so often the cloud tears open to show the jagged dolerite crown before closing again. It could be January. It could be July. In Tasmania's high country, the weather doesn't much care which month you picked.

That is a useful lesson for the whole country. Australia doesn't have one climate; it has several, running on different calendars. The month that is perfect for the Red Centre is miserable in Kakadu, and the one that suits the reef can be crowded and hot down south. Get the timing right for each region and the trip falls into place. Get it wrong and you spend it dodging floods, heat or stingers.

## The seasons run backwards, and not everywhere has four

The first adjustment for Northern Hemisphere travellers: the seasons are reversed. As [Tourism Australia's seasons guide](https://www.australia.com/en/facts-and-planning/when-to-go/australias-seasons.html) puts it, summer falls between December and February, so Christmas is a beach holiday and July is winter.

The second: four seasons only describe the south. The same Tourism Australia guide notes that the Top End and Tropical North Queensland have a wet season (November to April) and a dry season (May to October) instead. The Bureau of Meteorology's [climate classification maps](https://www.bom.gov.au/climate/maps/averages/climate-classification/) show the same split another way, dividing the country into zones by how rain falls between the warm months (November to April) and the cool months (May to October), from "summer dominant" zones with a marked wet summer and dry winter to winter-rainfall and arid zones.

Put simply, you are choosing between three broad patterns: the tropical north, the arid centre and the temperate south. Most first trips mix all three, which is why the month you travel shapes the route more than almost anything else.

## The tropical north: wet and dry

In the Top End, the Kimberley and Tropical North Queensland, the dry season is the classic travel window. Tourism Australia describes it as clear blue skies, warm days and cool nights, with many visitors favouring the end of the wet or the start of the dry, when the country is still green.

The wet is a different place. Tourism Australia describes very hot days, high humidity, monsoonal rains and the possibility of cyclones. It is also when waterfalls are at their most dramatic, storms roll in most afternoons and there are far fewer people. It is a genuine trade-off rather than a no-go, but you need flexibility, because roads and sites can close at short notice.

### Kakadu's six seasons

Kakadu is the best place to see how thin the "wet and dry" labels really are. Parks Australia's [Kakadu seasons page](https://kakadu.gov.au/discover/nature/seasons/) explains that while most visitors know northern Australia's two seasons, the park's Traditional Owners recognise six, marked by changes in weather, flowering plants and bush foods:

- **Kudjewk**, the monsoon season: December to March.
- **Bangkerreng**, the "knock 'em down" storm season: April.
- **Yekke**, the cooler but still humid season: May to mid-June.
- **Wurrkeng**, the cold weather season: mid-June to mid-August.
- **Kurrung**, hot dry weather: mid-August to mid-October.
- **Kunumeleng**, the pre-monsoon storm season: mid-October to late December.

For practical planning, the park's [when to come page](https://kakadu.gov.au/plan/when-to-come/) calls May to October the most popular time, with most visitor sites open and accommodation worth booking ahead. In the tropical summer, it says, flooding closes a number of attractions, though Burrungkuy (Nourlangie) and Yellow Water stay open all year, and scenic flights show the waterfalls when ground access is cut.

Site openings vary year to year. The [Kakadu access report](https://kakadu.gov.au/news/access-and-alerts/), for example, lists the Jim Jim and Twin Falls area as closed throughout 2026. Check it before you build a trip around any one site. Our guide to [Kakadu and the Top End in the dry season](/stories/kakadu-top-end-dry-season/) goes into the detail.

## The centre: heat, cold nights and wildflowers

The Red Centre runs on a simpler rule: avoid the heat. Parks Australia's [Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa when to come page](https://uluru.gov.au/plan/when-to-come/) says the best time is between May and September, when daytime maximums are usually between 20°C and 30°C and walking is safer and more pleasant. It also warns that winter nights can be very cold, so pack warm layers, and notes that August and September are usually the best months for wildflowers.

From October to March, the same page says, daytime temperatures regularly exceed 35°C. Summer storms can fill the waterholes and send water streaming off Uluṟu, which is a rare sight, but the park asks summer visitors to drink plenty of water and not walk after 11am. It also mentions the flies, and suggests a head net. Read our guide to [visiting Uluru and the Red Centre well](/stories/uluru-red-centre-how-to-visit/) before you go.

## The reef and stinger season

The Great Barrier Reef can be visited year round, and Tourism Australia notes that it is often calm in the wet season. The catch is marine stingers.

Surf Life Saving Australia's [tropical stingers guidance](https://beachsafe.org.au/surf-safety/tropical-stingers) describes stinger season as generally November to March. Its advice is to swim at patrolled beaches between the red and yellow flags, inside stinger nets where they are provided, to obey safety signs, and to wear a full-body lycra suit, particularly during the season. For tropical stings, it says to call Triple Zero (000), give emergency care and pour vinegar on the affected area.

In practice, that means summer reef trips are perfectly doable with a stinger suit on, while the drier months from around winter into spring bring lower humidity and more comfortable days on land. For more on choosing a base and a season, see [the Great Barrier Reef: where to go and when](/stories/great-barrier-reef-where-and-when/).

## The south: Tasmania, Victoria and South Australia

The southern states are where the four seasons really show.

**Summer** is peak time. Long evenings, beach weather in South Australia and along Victoria's coast, and the best chance of settled days for walking in Tasmania. It also brings crowds, higher demand for accommodation and bushfire risk. Tasmania's Parks and Wildlife Service, on its [safety in parks page](https://parks.tas.gov.au/safety-in-parks), asks visitors to check fire weather and current fire activity before heading out.

**Autumn** is our favourite compromise: settled weather, softer light, fewer people and turning leaves in the cooler highlands.

**Winter** is short days, cold mornings and grey weather on the coast, but also quiet roads, fires in country pubs and snow on Tasmania's mountains. If you are walking, take the warnings seriously: the same Tasmanian safety page says snow, hail, rain, wind and sun are all possible at any time of the year, that summer snowfalls in the high country are not uncommon, and that under-equipped walkers have died.

**Spring** brings wildflowers, young animals and lengthening days, with weather that can swing from warm to wintry in a day.

For the routes themselves, see [driving the Great Ocean Road](/stories/driving-the-great-ocean-road/) and [Tasmania in a week](/stories/tasmania-in-a-week/).

## The west coast and Ningaloo

Western Australia stretches from the tropical Kimberley to the temperate south-west, so treat it as several regions. The north follows the wet and dry pattern; Tourism Australia includes Broome among the places that get heavy rain and storms in the wet season.

Ningaloo is where timing matters most, because of the wildlife. The Department of Biodiversity, Conservation and Attractions' [Ningaloo Marine Park page](https://exploreparks.dbca.wa.gov.au/park/ningaloo-marine-park) says the best time to see whale sharks on their migration is between March and August, and that humpback whales are around from June to November. In February and March, it adds, you can watch turtle hatchlings head for the sea.

Combine those windows and the overlap of the whale shark and humpback seasons looks especially tempting. Our trip along [Western Australia's coast from Perth to Exmouth](/stories/western-australia-coast-perth-to-exmouth/) puts it into a route.

## School holidays and long weekends

Weather is only half the story. Australian families travel in school holidays, and they can fill campgrounds, coastal towns, flights and family rooms well ahead.

Dates vary by state, so check the official calendar for the states you're visiting. Victoria's [school term dates page](https://www.vic.gov.au/school-term-dates-and-holidays-victoria), for example, shows its summer holidays running from 21 December 2026 to 27 January 2027, with shorter breaks between the terms in April, mid-year and late September to early October. Other states follow a broadly similar rhythm, but not on identical dates.

Public holidays add pressure too, especially Easter, Christmas and New Year and the long weekends each state declares. If your trip lands on one, book accommodation and popular tours early, and expect busier roads.

## When to go where: a round-up

Treat these as starting points and check the linked official pages, because seasons shift and park access changes from year to year.

- **Sydney:** good year round. Summer is beach season and busy; autumn and spring are often the most comfortable for walking the city and the Blue Mountains.
- **Melbourne and the Great Ocean Road:** summer and autumn for the coast; winter is quieter and moodier, with shorter days for driving.
- **Tasmania:** summer for walking and long evenings, autumn for colour and calm; winter for snow and solitude, with proper gear.
- **South Australia:** spring and autumn for comfortable touring; summer for the beaches, with heat and fire danger to watch.
- **Red Centre:** May to September for walking; October to March is very hot, so walk early and finish by late morning.
- **Top End and Kakadu:** the dry season, roughly May to October, for access; the wet for storms, waterfalls from the air and few people.
- **Great Barrier Reef:** any time, but wear a stinger suit in the summer months and check conditions locally.
- **Ningaloo and the Coral Coast:** March to August for whale sharks, June to November for humpbacks.
