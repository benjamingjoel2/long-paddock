The light goes first. On a Tasmanian evening the sun drops behind the hills, the grass turns from gold to grey, and the paddock that looked empty an hour ago starts to move. A wombat trundles out of the scrub like a small, determined barrel. Pademelons lift their heads from the grass. Somewhere behind you a currawong calls, and you realise you have stopped talking.

That is the real reward of wildlife watching in Australia: not a guaranteed sighting through glass, but animals going about their own lives in their own places. It takes a little planning, some patience at dawn and dusk, and a willingness to keep your distance. Here is where we would start.

## Kangaroo Island: sea lions at Seal Bay

Kangaroo Island, off the coast of South Australia, is the kind of place where wildlife is part of the drive. The headline stop is Seal Bay Conservation Park, where the [National Parks and Wildlife Service South Australia](https://www.parks.sa.gov.au/experiences/seal-bay) describes an Australian sea lion colony that has lived there for thousands of years. A wheelchair-accessible boardwalk of roughly 900 metres return runs through the dunes to viewing platforms over the beach, and guided tours explain the endangered sea lions from a safe distance.

One important note: at the time of writing, the same page carries an alert that access to the beach has been temporarily paused to help protect the colony, with the boardwalk remaining open and a roaming guide on hand. Conditions like this change, so check the Seal Bay page before you plan your day around a beach walk.

Give yourself more than a day on the island. The roads are long, the light at either end of the day is when most animals are moving, and you will want time to slow down.

## Tasmania: wombats, wallabies and the elusive devil

If we had to pick one state for wildlife, Tasmania would be close to the top. Three parks stand out.

### Maria Island

Reached by passenger ferry from Triabunna on the east coast, [Maria Island National Park](https://parks.tas.gov.au/explore-our-parks/maria-island-national-park) is the place where wildlife watching feels almost effortless; the park says that wherever you are on the island, you are going to see wildlife. Tasmania Parks and Wildlife Service says you are likely to spot forester kangaroos, Bennett's wallabies, Flinders Island wombats, Cape Barren geese and native hens alongside the pademelons that have always lived there. Tasmanian devils were introduced to the island in 2012 as part of a captive-breeding effort after the species declined across the state from Devil Facial Tumour Disease. The service asks visitors to keep at least 2 metres from all wildlife, and a parks pass is required.

### Narawntapu

On the north coast, [Narawntapu National Park](https://parks.tas.gov.au/explore-our-parks/narawntapu-national-park) is easier to reach and, at the right hour, just as rewarding. The open grassland around the Springlawn visitor area is where forester kangaroos, Bennett's wallabies and pademelons graze, and the park notes that it comes alive with marsupials at dusk. Bring a warm layer and sit still.

### Cradle Mountain

[Cradle Mountain](https://parks.tas.gov.au/explore-our-parks/cradle-mountain) is better known for its walks, but Tasmania Parks and Wildlife Service lists Tasmanian devils, spotted-tailed and eastern quolls, wombats, platypus and echidnas among its residents. Platypus are worth looking for in quiet water early and late in the day; patience matters more than luck. Wombats are the easiest win. A parks pass is required, along with a shuttle bus ticket in operating hours.

A word on devils. They are nocturnal, which the service's [caring for wildlife](https://parks.tas.gov.au/discovery-and-learning/wildlife/caring-for-wildlife) page points out, and numbers have fallen because of disease. Seeing one in the wild is a real stroke of fortune, not something to count on. If you want a reliable sighting, a reputable wildlife sanctuary is the honest answer. For the rest of the island, our [Tasmania in a week](/stories/tasmania-in-a-week/) itinerary fits these parks into a loop.

## Phillip Island: penguins at sunset, koalas by day

Within reach of Melbourne, Phillip Island is home to the Penguin Parade, run by Phillip Island Nature Parks. The [Penguin Parade page](https://www.penguins.org.au/attractions/penguin-parade/) says the little penguins arrive at sunset every evening and that bookings are essential, so reserve ahead, especially in summer. Arrival times shift through the year; the page links to an arrival calendar, so check it before you go.

By day, the same organisation runs the [Koala Conservation Reserve](https://www.penguins.org.au/attractions/koala-conservation-reserve/), where treetop boardwalks let you watch koalas in their natural bushland habitat rather than in a pen. Both pages also ask drivers to take care on the island at dawn, dusk and after dark, when wildlife is most active. Phillip Island pairs well with [the Great Ocean Road](/stories/driving-the-great-ocean-road/) if you are spending time in Victoria.

## Ningaloo: whale sharks and humpbacks

On the far side of the country, Ningaloo Reef runs close to the shore of Western Australia's Coral Coast. The state parks agency, the Department of Biodiversity, Conservation and Attractions, says on its [Ningaloo Marine Park page](https://exploreparks.dbca.wa.gov.au/park/ningaloo-marine-park) that the best time to catch whale sharks on their migration is between March and August, and humpback whales are around from June to November. It suggests organising a guided experience to swim with whale sharks, humpbacks, manta rays and other marine life, and notes that turtle hatchlings make their first dash to the sea in the warmer months of February and March, when you can visit the Jurabi Turtle Centre.

The overlap between the two seasons, roughly June to August, is when you have the best chance of seeing both giants on one trip. Go with an established tour operator who will brief you on how to approach these animals before you get in the water. Our guide to [Western Australia's coast from Perth to Exmouth](/stories/western-australia-coast-perth-to-exmouth/) covers the drive north.

## Mon Repos: turtles on the beach at night

On the southern end of the Great Barrier Reef coast, near Bundaberg in Queensland, Mon Repos is one of the most moving wildlife experiences in the country: a turtle hauling herself up the sand in the dark to lay her eggs, or a scramble of hatchlings heading for the surf.

Access is by ranger-led tour only. The [Queensland Government](https://statements.qld.gov.au/statements/106207) says the guided night-time Turtle Encounters are led by Queensland Parks and Wildlife Service rangers, that Mon Repos is recognised as one of the most important loggerhead turtle nesting sites in the South Pacific, and that the 2026–27 season runs from 7 November 2026 to 21 March 2027. Tickets are booked in advance, and dates are set each season, so check before you plan. For the reef itself, see our guide to [the Great Barrier Reef](/stories/great-barrier-reef-where-and-when/).

## Crocodiles in the Top End

In the Top End, wildlife watching comes with a serious edge. Saltwater crocodiles are dangerous, and Kakadu's [Be Crocwise safety advice](https://kakadu.gov.au/plan/plan-your-trip/staying-safe/) says they inhabit most areas of water in the park, including floodplains, billabongs, creeks, rivers and the coast. Always obey warning signs, and where there are none, assume crocodiles are present.

If you want to see one up close, do it with an experienced guide. On your own, keep well back from the water's edge, never wade, never clean fish or prepare food at the water, and never feed or interfere with a crocodile. The same page asks for extra care during the breeding season from November to April. If the Top End is on your list, our [Kakadu dry season guide](/stories/kakadu-top-end-dry-season/) has the details.

## How to watch wildlife well

The best wildlife encounters are the ones where the animal barely notices you. A few rules make all the difference:

- **Don't feed anything.** Tasmania Parks and Wildlife Service explains on its [caring for wildlife](https://parks.tas.gov.au/discovery-and-learning/wildlife/caring-for-wildlife) page that fed animals can become bold and injure people, that processed food can cause a painful jaw condition in wallabies, and that hand-feeding can leave animals dependent and at risk of starvation when visitors leave.
- **Keep your distance and never touch.** The same page asks for a minimum of 2 metres from native animals. Seals, sea lions, penguins and whales have their own viewing guidelines; follow them and your guide's instructions.
- **Drive slowly from dusk to dawn.** Native animals are most active early and late in the day, and long stretches of Tasmania's roads pass through their habitat. Tasmania Parks and Wildlife Service urges drivers to slow down between dusk and dawn. Plan to arrive before dark rather than racing the sunset.
- **If you hit or find an injured animal**, stop only if it is safe. In Tasmania, the [caring for wildlife page](https://parks.tas.gov.au/discovery-and-learning/wildlife/caring-for-wildlife) lists Bonorong Wildlife Rescue on 0447 264 625 and notes that female marsupials may have pouch young that can be saved. In other states, contact the local park office or a local wildlife rescue group; numbers change, so look them up before your trip.
- **Leave the drone at home.** Tasmania Parks and Wildlife Service states that recreational use of drones is not permitted in national parks and reserves it manages, as shown on the [Cradle Mountain page](https://parks.tas.gov.au/explore-our-parks/cradle-mountain). Rules vary between parks and states, so check before you fly anywhere near wildlife.

Above all, go slowly. Sit by a clearing at dusk, wait on a beach after dark, scan a riverbank from a safe distance. Australia's animals reward the people who give them time.
