The first thing you notice is the quiet. Then the colour: the sand at your feet is the red of rust and brick dust, and the spinifex is a pale, prickly gold. Before dawn the air is cold enough for a jacket, and somewhere to the east a long dark shape begins to separate itself from the sky. By the time the sun clears the horizon, Uluṟu is glowing, every fold and cave in its flanks picked out in shadow, and the flies have woken up too.

People come a very long way for that moment, and it is worth it. But Uluṟu is not just a view. It is the centre of a living cultural landscape that belongs to Aṉangu, the park's Traditional Owners, and the trip goes best when you arrive knowing a little about how to behave as a guest. This guide covers the practical side, from passes and timing to the long roads in between, with the park's own requests front and centre.

## Before you go: passes and the rules that matter

Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa National Park is jointly managed by Aṉangu and Parks Australia, and every adult visitor needs a park pass. You can [buy your pass online through the park's official site](https://uluru.gov.au/plan/buy-your-pass/), save it to your phone, and scan it at the entry station; if you can't buy online, the same page says you can pick one up at the entry station on the way in. Under-18s go free. We won't quote the fee here because it changes, so check that page before you travel.

Note that this pass covers Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa only. The [same Parks Australia page](https://uluru.gov.au/plan/buy-your-pass/) explains that other national parks in the Northern Territory, including the ones we mention further down, are run by the Northern Territory Government, which has its own entry and booking requirements through its NT Park Bookings website. Sort both before you leave home.

### The climb is closed, and why

You may have older relatives who climbed Uluṟu. You won't. [The climb closed permanently on 26 October 2019](https://uluru.gov.au/discover/history/uluru-climb-closure/), on the anniversary of the park's handback to Aṉangu. Parks Australia explains that climbing Uluṟu was not generally permitted under Tjukurpa, Aṉangu law and Culture, and that Aṉangu had long asked visitors not to climb. The park's Board of Management voted unanimously for the closure, and the same page notes that attempting to climb is now a breach of federal environment law, with penalties issued.

What replaces the climb is better anyway: walking around the base, close to the rock, with time to look properly.

### Photography: what not to shoot

This is the rule most visitors don't know about. Some parts of Uluṟu and Kata Tjuṯa are culturally sensitive sites, and the park asks that you [do not photograph them at all](https://uluru.gov.au/things-do/activities/photography/). The rock features at these places hold knowledge meant to be seen only in place and by particular people, so images of them shouldn't circulate elsewhere. Signs mark the sensitive areas, and the park publishes maps.

The north-east face of Uluṟu, the side most people see from the resort and from the air, is one of the most sensitive. According to the [park's photography guidance](https://uluru.gov.au/things-do/activities/photography/), Aṉangu ask that you take only wide shots of it from a distance, and they suggest shooting an hour or two before sunset, when shadows naturally hide the sacred details. A few other points from the same page:

- Always ask before photographing Aṉangu.
- Drones are prohibited in the park.
- Using photos or video for commercial purposes or public display, which the park says includes bloggers, influencers and content creators, needs a media permit.

## Start at the Cultural Centre

Parks Australia suggests [starting your trip at the Cultural Centre](https://uluru.gov.au/things-do/cultural-centre/), and we agree. It sits on the main road to Uluṟu, a short drive in from the entry station, in a building made of locally made mud bricks. Inside are exhibits on Aṉangu Culture and the park's natural world, an information desk staffed by rangers, and two Aboriginal art galleries, Maruku Arts and Walkatjara Art. The park recommends allowing at least two hours, and suggests the Tjukurpa Tunnel as your very first experience of the park.

Go on your first morning, before you walk anywhere. The stories you hear there change what you see at the base of the rock: the marks and hollows stop being geology and start being the tracks of ancestral beings. It is also the one place in the park to buy food and drinks, at the Ininti café, which is worth knowing on a long day.

## Kata Tjuṯa and the heat

Kata Tjuṯa, the cluster of great domed rocks to the west of Uluṟu, deserves at least a morning of its own. It is a place of great significance to Aṉangu, and you are welcome to walk it, but only on marked tracks. The two walks most people do are Waḻpa Gorge and the Valley of the Winds.

The Valley of the Winds is where heat management becomes real. The [park's access report](https://uluru.gov.au/news/access-and-alerts/) shows that the full circuit closes from 11am on days when the maximum temperature hits 36°C. This is not a suggestion; the track is shut. Check the access report the evening before, start at first light, and treat any closure as a gift of a slower afternoon.

Heat is the single biggest risk in the park. The [Staying safe page](https://uluru.gov.au/plan/plan-your-trip/staying-safe/) is blunt about it: temperatures can exceed 40°C in summer, and heat exhaustion, dehydration and hyponatremia are real dangers. Its advice includes:

- Walk in the cooler parts of the day; in summer, the park strongly recommends not walking after 11am.
- Carry and drink at least one litre of water per person per hour of walking, as [Parks Australia advises](https://uluru.gov.au/plan/plan-your-trip/staying-safe/).
- Wear a broad-brimmed hat, sunscreen and sturdy shoes, walk with someone else, and stay on marked tracks.
- Don't walk on any track that has been closed for heat.

## When to come

Parks Australia says the [best time to come is between May and September](https://uluru.gov.au/plan/when-to-come/), and for a first visit we agree: crisp mornings, warm days, and proper walking weather. Winter nights can be very cold, so pack layers as well as a sunhat. From October to March the heat builds, and while the same page notes that summer storms can fill the waterholes and set Uluṟu's waterfalls running, you will be fitting your walking into early mornings and accepting closures. Summer also brings flies; a head net earns its place in your bag. August and September are usually the best months for wildflowers.

Whenever you go, plan for at least three nights in the area. One sunrise and one sunset is not enough; the rock changes all day.

## Getting there: fly in or drive

Most people fly. Yulara, the resort town just outside the park, [has its own airport, Ayers Rock (AYQ)](https://uluru.gov.au/plan/getting-here/), with direct flights from several capital cities. From overseas you'll usually connect through one of those cities. The park notes that rental cars at Yulara are limited, so book ahead if you want to drive yourself.

The alternative is to drive from Alice Springs. [Parks Australia puts it at](https://uluru.gov.au/plan/getting-here/) roughly five and a half hours direct, but allow a full day once you add fuel stops, rest breaks and the inevitable roadside photographs. Many travellers fold this into the Red Centre Way, a multi-day drive taking in Uluṟu, Watarrka and Tjoritja.

## Alice Springs, Tjoritja and Watarrka

If you have five nights or more, give some of them to the country around Alice Springs, known in Arrernte as Mparntwe. The town is the natural base for the ranges to its west, a place to restock, and a good spot to spend time in Aboriginal art galleries before or after the long drives.

West of town, Tjoritja, the West MacDonnell Ranges, runs out in a long line of red ridges cut by gorges and waterholes. Ormiston Gorge is the one most people remember: cool water at the foot of glowing cliffs, ghost gums leaning out of the rock. Come early, take water, and stay on the marked tracks.

Watarrka National Park, home to Kings Canyon, lies west of the direct road between Alice and Uluṟu and makes a natural overnight stop on the Red Centre Way. The walk up and around the canyon rim is the reason to come, with views straight down sheer sandstone walls. Start it at dawn; the heat advice above applies here just as much.

Both Tjoritja and Watarrka are Northern Territory Government parks, separate from Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa, so check their entry and booking requirements via the link on the [Uluṟu pass page](https://uluru.gov.au/plan/buy-your-pass/) before you go.

## Driving in the outback

Outback driving is a pleasure, and it is also where visitors get caught out. Distances are long, services are few, and the road is shared with road trains and wildlife. The Northern Territory's [road safety advice](https://northernterritory.com/plan/useful-information/safety-information/roads) covers the essentials, and we'd underline these:

- **Check the road report daily.** Conditions change quickly, especially after rain. The [Road Report NT](https://roadreport.nt.gov.au/home) is the official source, and the Uluṟu access report points to it for roads around the park.
- **Fuel and water.** Don't rely on fuel being available in remote areas, fill up whenever you can, and carry extra water. The [NT guidance](https://northernterritory.com/plan/useful-information/safety-information/roads) recommends four to six litres per person per day for long drives, and says to stay with your vehicle if you break down.
- **Avoid driving at night.** Many NT roads are unfenced, and kangaroos, cattle, camels and horses are most active around sunrise, sunset and after dark. [The same page](https://northernterritory.com/plan/useful-information/safety-information/roads) advises slowing down and not swerving to avoid animals.
- **Read your hire agreement.** Rental companies may restrict where you can take their vehicles, including unsealed roads, so check before you plan any detour off the bitumen.
- **Give road trains room.** They are very long, and overtaking one takes far more road than you expect.

Rules and closures change, so check the linked pages before you set off rather than relying on what you read here.

## Making the most of it

The best Red Centre trips are unhurried. Walk the base of Uluṟu in the cool of the morning. Sit through a sunset without looking at your phone. Stay on the marked tracks.
