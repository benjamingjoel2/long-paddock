North of Perth the city thins out quickly. The suburbs give way to banksia scrub and pale dunes, the Indian Ocean flashes in and out on your left, and the light gets harder and whiter with every hour. By the time you reach the reef at Ningaloo the colours have changed completely: red earth, white sand, and water so clear that the coral shows from the beach. In between lies one of the great coastal drives in Australia. It is also one of the longest, and that is the first thing to plan around.

## The shape of the trip, and how many days

The route is simple: follow the coast north from Perth through Nambung National Park and the Pinnacles, on to Kalbarri, then out to Shark Bay. From there head north to Coral Bay and finally Exmouth, at the tip of the North West Cape. Plenty of travellers then fly back to Perth from Learmonth, near Exmouth, rather than drive the whole way again.

Perth to Exmouth by this route is well over a thousand kilometres. You can drive it in a few long days, but you would see almost nothing. We would give it **ten days at the very least, and two weeks if you can**. That allows a night or two at Kalbarri, two or three around Shark Bay, and at least four on the Ningaloo coast, which is where most people wish they had more time.

A rough plan for 12 to 14 days:

- **Days 1–2:** Perth to the Pinnacles and the Turquoise Coast, then on to Kalbarri
- **Days 3–4:** Kalbarri National Park
- **Days 5–7:** Shark Bay: Shell Beach, Hamelin Pool, Monkey Mia and around Denham
- **Days 8–9:** Coral Bay
- **Days 10–14:** Exmouth, Cape Range National Park and Ningaloo Marine Park

## The Pinnacles and Kalbarri

The first real stop is Nambung National Park, a few hours north of Perth and the traditional country of the Yued people. Limestone spires by the thousand rise out of yellow sand in the Pinnacles Desert, and at sunrise or late afternoon their shadows stretch out long across the dunes. There is a loop drive and walking trails. Nearby Lake Thetis is your first look at stromatolites, which you will meet again at Shark Bay. In late winter and spring the heath around the park flowers.

Further north, Kalbarri National Park is Nanda country. The Murchison River has cut a deep gorge through banded red and white sandstone, and the [Kalbarri Skywalk](https://exploreparks.dbca.wa.gov.au/park/kalbarri-national-park) puts two platforms out over the edge. Go early, before the heat and the tour buses. Along the coast, the sea cliffs and rock formations south of town are a good second half to the day.

Take Kalbarri's heat seriously. DBCA says there is no drinking water in the park, that summer temperatures can be extreme, and recommends planning hikes for the cooler months of May to October. It also closes the Loop Trail after 7am from November to March. Carry more water than you think you need, and start walking at first light.

## Shark Bay

Shark Bay is where the trip slows down. The [Shark Bay World Heritage Area](https://www.dbca.wa.gov.au/management/world-heritage-areas/shark-bay-world-heritage-area) is best known, in DBCA's words, for its dugongs, its vast seagrass beds and the most diverse and abundant living marine stromatolites in the world. It is Malgana country. It is also a place of huge skies, red cliffs dropping into turquoise shallows, and an almost total absence of hurry.

Three stops stand out:

- **Shell Beach.** A beach made of tiny white shells instead of sand, piled in deep ridges along the shore. The water is clear and shallow, and entry is free.
- **Hamelin Pool.** The stromatolites here are rock-like "living fossils" that DBCA says give a glimpse of how the planet looked billions of years ago. Note that the [viewing boardwalk at Hamelin Pool is closed until further notice](https://exploreparks.dbca.wa.gov.au/park/hamelin-pool-marine-nature-reserve) after cyclone damage, though the walk trail and other attractions remain open. Never walk on the stromatolites, and check the page before you go.
- **Monkey Mia.** Wild dolphins swim in just metres from the shore here. DBCA describes a managed experience in which a small group of dolphins is offered a snack of local fish, up to three times a day, and only a little so they keep foraging naturally. Arrive early, follow the staff's instructions, and never feed or touch the dolphins yourself. Monkey Mia has its own reserve fee, which a park pass does not cover.

Give Shark Bay at least two nights. Denham makes a comfortable base, and a cruise or a guided tour on the water is the best chance of seeing dugongs.

## Coral Bay and the reef

From Shark Bay the land turns drier and redder as you head north, and the next town, Coral Bay, is little more than a horseshoe of beach, a few places to stay and the reef just offshore. Ningaloo is a fringing reef, which means it sits close to the coast. At Coral Bay you can wade in from the sand and float over coral within minutes. DBCA has a marked kayak trail here with buoys to tie off to while you snorkel, and boat tours run out to deeper water.

Two nights is ideal: one day in the water from the beach, one on a boat.

## Exmouth, Cape Range and Ningaloo

Exmouth is the base for the northern end of Ningaloo Marine Park and for [Cape Range National Park](https://exploreparks.dbca.wa.gov.au/park/cape-range-national-park), the traditional country of the Baiyungu, Thalanyji and Yinigurdira peoples. On one side of the cape, rugged limestone ranges are cut by canyons; on the other, a long string of beaches runs along the reef. Turquoise Bay, Lakeside and Oyster Stacks are the snorkelling sites everyone talks about. At Yardie Creek you can walk the gorge or take a boat up between the cliffs, keeping an eye out for rock-wallabies.

The heat here is serious too. DBCA warns that [Cape Range temperatures](https://exploreparks.dbca.wa.gov.au/park/cape-range-national-park) can soar above 45°C in summer, and that walks should only be attempted between April and September.

**Camping in Cape Range.** If you want to sleep by the reef, book well ahead. DBCA says that at campgrounds such as [Osprey Bay](https://exploreparks.dbca.wa.gov.au/site/osprey-bay-campground) bookings are essential and must be made before you arrive. Ningaloo coast bookings open monthly on a set schedule, and demand for the peak season is very high. Some coastal campgrounds have also had cyclone damage and closures, so check the park page for current notices.

## Whale sharks, humpbacks and turtles

The reason many people make this drive is the whale sharks. DBCA says they are [most reliably seen at Ningaloo from March to July](https://www.dbca.wa.gov.au/wildlife-and-ecosystems/marine/whale-shark-management-western-australia), and in some years they stay into August and beyond. Seeing them means a day on the water: only operators licensed by DBCA are allowed to run commercial whale shark tours, and DBCA has legislated interaction protocols for swimmers and boats around the sharks. Book early in the season, and expect that the sea and the sharks set the terms on the day. You will be swimming in open water, so you need to be comfortable with a snorkel.

Humpback whales pass along the coast on their annual migration, which DBCA puts at roughly May to October. DBCA has also allowed licensed operators to run in-water humpback tours, so ask what is on offer the year you go; plenty of people are just as happy watching from a boat, a beach or a lookout.

Turtles also nest on this coast. DBCA lists beaches such as Hunters, near the tip of the cape, as turtle rookeries in season, and notes that in the warmer months of February and March you can watch hatchlings make their first dash to the sea. The Jurabi Turtle Centre, north of Exmouth, is the place to learn about them and join an eco-education tour. Keep your distance and follow the signs on nesting beaches.

## On the road: distance, heat and cyclones

**Distance and supplies.** Towns get further apart the further north you go. [Main Roads WA advises](https://www.mainroads.wa.gov.au/travel-information/driving-in-wa/long-distance-driving/) carrying enough food and water to last two to three days beyond your planned travel time, extra fuel on long stretches, and planning your trip so you drive only in daylight. Fill up whenever you can, even if the tank is half full.

**Road trains.** Main Roads notes that road trains, which can be enormously long, are common on regional roads. Give them room, do not cut in front of them, and only overtake on a long, clear straight.

**Wildlife and fatigue.** Kangaroos, emus and stray livestock are a real hazard, particularly at dawn and dusk when animals are most active. Slow down when you see them near the road, and be ready to brake rather than swerve. Fatigue is the other quiet danger on long, straight roads: stop often and share the driving.

**Heat.** From late spring to autumn the heat inland can be fierce. Start early, carry plenty of water, and do not walk in the middle of the day.

**Cyclones and road closures.** The Bureau of Meteorology says [the official tropical cyclone season runs from 1 November to 30 April](https://www.bom.gov.au/climate/cyclones/australia/), and the Ningaloo coast is in cyclone country. Main Roads warns that the north's wet season, generally November to April, can flood and close roads. Check BoM warnings and the [Main Roads Travel Map](https://travelmap.mainroads.wa.gov.au/) before each long drive.

**Park passes.** [Entry fees apply at more than 30 of Western Australia's parks](https://exploreparks.dbca.wa.gov.au/park-passes), including Nambung, Kalbarri and Cape Range on this route. DBCA sells holiday passes for road trips that cover entry for a private vehicle. Pass types and prices change, so buy through the official site.

## When to go

For most travellers the sweet spot is roughly **April to October**. The heat eases, the whale sharks arrive and then the humpbacks, and it is the season DBCA recommends for walking in Kalbarri and Cape Range. It is also peak season on the Ningaloo coast, so book campsites, accommodation and boat tours early.

Late winter and spring add wildflowers in the south of the route. The summer months bring turtle hatchlings, quieter beaches and fierce heat, inside the cyclone season. Whenever you go, check the park alerts, road conditions and weather warnings on the official pages linked above before you set off, because conditions change.
