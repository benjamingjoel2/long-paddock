The boat leaves the marina while the light is still soft and grey-gold, and for the first hour the sea looks like any other sea. Then the colour changes. The deep navy breaks into patches of turquoise and olive, a line of white water marks a reef edge, and someone on the deck goes quiet. When you finally slip off the stern in your stinger suit, the sound drops away to your own breathing, and below you the coral runs off in every direction: plates, branches, brains, the flick of a parrotfish, a turtle that couldn't care less that you've arrived.

That moment is what most people come for. Getting it right, though, depends less on luck than on two decisions you make long before you board: where you base yourself, and when you go.

## A reef the length of a coastline

The first thing to understand is that the Great Barrier Reef is not one place. It is a long system of separate reefs, cays and islands strung along the Queensland coast, and the experience changes a great deal from one end to the other. [Tourism Australia notes](https://www.australia.com/en/places/cairns-and-surrounds/guide-to-the-great-barrier-reef.html) that access points are scattered between Cape York in the far north and Bundaberg in the south, and lists Cairns, Townsville, Proserpine (for Airlie Beach), Bundaberg and Hamilton Island as the main airports for reaching it.

So "going to the reef" really means choosing a gateway. For most first-time visitors that comes down to three options.

### Cairns and Port Douglas

This is the classic base, and for good reason. Cairns has the widest choice of reef trips; Tourism Australia's [guide to choosing a tour](https://www.australia.com/en/places/cairns-and-surrounds/how-to-choose-the-right-great-barrier-reef-tour-to-suit-you.html) calls it one of the closest mainland jumping-off points, with the largest selection of tours. Port Douglas, roughly an hour's drive north, is quieter and puts you within reach of the Daintree rainforest as well as the reef. If you want rainforest and reef in one trip, this is where they sit side by side.

### The Whitsundays and Airlie Beach

Further south, the reef experience is as much about islands and sailing as it is about coral. Tourism Australia's [Whitsundays guide](https://www.australia.com/en/places/whitsundays-and-surrounds/guide-to-the-whitsundays.html) describes skippered and bareboat charters, Whitehaven Beach, snorkelling and diving trips, and helicopter flights over Heart Reef. You fly into Hamilton Island or the Whitsunday Coast Airport near Airlie Beach. Choose this base if the idea of a few days on a yacht, anchoring in calm bays, appeals more than daily runs out of a single town.

### The southern reef: Lady Elliot and Heron

At the southern end, the reef comes close to small coral cays you can actually stay on. Tourism Australia's [Southern Great Barrier Reef guide](https://www.australia.com/en/places/brisbane-and-surrounds/guide-to-the-southern-great-barrier-reef.html) names Bundaberg as the gateway to the region and lists Heron Island and Lady Elliot Island among its destinations, along with turtles, manta rays and humpbacks. Staying on a cay changes the rhythm completely: the reef is off the beach, not a boat ride away, and the day-trippers are gone by evening.

## Outer reef, inner reef or island?

Once you have a base, the next choice is what kind of reef day you want. Broadly:

- **Outer reef trips** run further offshore to reefs on the edge of the system. These usually mean a longer boat ride but clearer water and a stronger sense of being properly out at sea. Some operators use permanently moored pontoons; others take smaller groups to a rotating set of sites.
- **Inner reef and coral cay trips** are closer to shore and often calmer, which suits nervous snorkellers and families. Water clarity can be more variable, especially after rain.
- **Island stays** trade the daily commute for time. Snorkelling straight off the beach at dawn, with no schedule, is a different experience from a day trip.

Pontoons are worth a word. Tourism Australia's [tour guide](https://www.australia.com/en/places/cairns-and-surrounds/how-to-choose-the-right-great-barrier-reef-tour-to-suit-you.html) recommends operators with permanently moored pontoons for first-time divers, describing easy entry via steps to a submerged platform where you can stand waist-deep before lowering yourself in. They are busier than small boats, but for an uncertain swimmer that stable platform is reassuring. Experienced divers, by contrast, will get more from a liveaboard to the remote ribbon reefs.

## When to go: the dry months and the wet

The reef is open all year, but the seasons shape what you will find. [Tourism Australia's overview](https://www.australia.com/en/places/cairns-and-surrounds/guide-to-the-great-barrier-reef.html) suggests May to October as a great time to visit, when the water is still warm enough for a swim and underwater visibility is at its best, with winter (June to August) the high season.

Those dry-season months bring calmer seas, lower humidity and more comfortable days on deck. In the Whitsundays, Tourism Australia notes that winter is also when [humpback whales arrive](https://www.australia.com/en/places/whitsundays-and-surrounds/guide-to-the-whitsundays.html) to give birth and socialise, and it names spring (September to November) as the high season there. The trade-off is crowds and full boats in Australian school holidays, so book early if your dates are fixed.

Summer is the wet season in the tropical north: heat, heavy humidity, afternoon downpours and the rainforest at its most lush. It is also cyclone season. The [Bureau of Meteorology](https://www.bom.gov.au/climate/cyclones/australia/) states that the official tropical cyclone season in the Australian region runs from 1 November to 30 April. A cyclone does not mean your trip is ruined, but boat days can be cancelled at short notice, and you should build slack into your plans, carry travel insurance that covers weather disruption, and watch the Bureau's warnings while you are there.

If you are weighing the reef against other parts of the country, our guide to [Australia's seasons](/stories/australia-seasons-when-to-go/) sets out when each region is at its best.

## Stingers, suits and enclosures

The warm months bring the reef's most important safety issue: marine stingers. The [Queensland Government](https://www.qld.gov.au/emergency/safety/recreation/dangerous-marine) warns that dangerous tropical stingers, including box jellyfish and Irukandji, are present in tropical Queensland waters all year round, but the risk is higher during the marine stinger season from November to May.

What that means in practice:

- **Wear the suit.** Reef boats hand out full-body lycra stinger suits; put one on every time you get in. The Queensland Government advises considering a full-body lycra suit, and [healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/accidents-and-injuries/bites-and-stings/sea-creature-stings) recommends a full-body stinger suit in tropical waters. It also protects you from sunburn on a long snorkel.
- **Swim inside the nets on the mainland.** Some patrolled beaches put up stinger nets in season. The [Queensland Government](https://www.qld.gov.au/emergency/safety/recreation/dangerous-marine) says to swim in the stinger nets where provided, swim at patrolled beaches between the red-and-yellow flags, and never enter the water when a beach is closed.
- **Know the first aid.** If someone is stung, the same page says to call Triple Zero (000), ask for an ambulance and pour vinegar on the sting while you wait, and notes that Irukandji symptoms can take 20 to 40 minutes to appear.

The same Queensland page also covers crocodiles, which live in fresh and salt water in northern Queensland: swim only in designated safe areas and obey every warning sign. Rules and advice can change, so check the linked pages before you go.

## Snorkelling if you are not a strong swimmer

You do not need to be a strong swimmer to see the reef, but you do need to be honest about it. Tell the crew before you get in; good operators will offer a flotation vest or noodle, a guided snorkel, or a glass-bottom boat or semi-submersible instead. Stay where the lookouts can see you, never snorkel alone, and get out if you feel tired, cold or short of breath. Tourism Australia's general [swimming advice](https://www.australia.com/en/facts-and-planning/health-and-safety/faq.html) applies here too: never swim alone, at night, or after drinking.

If you have a heart condition or are older and less active, talk to your doctor before the trip and tell the crew on the day. A pontoon with steps and handrails, or a calm inner-reef site, is a much gentler start than jumping off the back of a boat into open water.

## Choosing an operator and treading lightly

The reef is a protected marine park, and how you visit it matters. A few things to know:

- **There is a reef charge.** Visitors contribute to reef management through an environmental management charge applied to reef tours, as [Tourism Australia explains](https://www.australia.com/en/places/cairns-and-surrounds/sustainable-holiday-great-barrier-reef.html). Because it is tied to tours, ask your operator whether it is already included when you book.
- **Look for eco-certified and High Standard operators.** The same Tourism Australia page points travellers towards eco-certified and High Standard Operators that build education about the reef into the trip, and suggests looking out for Master Reef Guides on board.
- **Don't touch.** Avoid contact with coral (watch your fins), take nothing away, not even shells, and think about what is in your sunscreen before you get in.
- **Contribute what you see.** The free Eye on the Reef app, which Tourism Australia's [tour guide](https://www.australia.com/en/places/cairns-and-surrounds/how-to-choose-the-right-great-barrier-reef-tour-to-suit-you.html) describes, lets snorkellers and divers report things like coral bleaching or crown-of-thorns starfish to the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority.

The reef is also Sea Country for many Traditional Owner groups. Tours with Indigenous guides, such as those running out of Cairns, add a layer of story that no fish identification card can match.

## Is the reef still worth seeing?

It is a fair question, and it deserves a straight answer. The reef is under real pressure. The [Queensland Government](https://www.qld.gov.au/environment/coasts-waterways/reef/great-barrier-reef-world-heritage-listing) acknowledges concerns about the reef's declining long-term outlook, climate change and water quality, and says management efforts aim to address key threats to the reef's ability to adapt to a changing climate. Coral bleaching is part of that story.

What that means for you as a visitor is that condition varies from reef to reef and year to year. Some sites look tired; others are full of colour and life. A good operator chooses sites based on conditions, and a good guide will tell you honestly what you are looking at and why it matters. Going with your eyes open, and with an operator that takes the reef seriously, is not a reason to stay away. If anything, it is a reason to go now, and to go well.
