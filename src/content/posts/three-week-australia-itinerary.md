The first morning in the Blue Mountains usually starts with a mistake: you step out to the lookout too early, in a T-shirt, and the cold off the escarpment goes straight through you. Then the haze lifts out of the valley, the eucalypts turn from grey to blue-green, and a currawong starts up somewhere below the cliff edge. It is a few hours from Sydney Harbour, and it feels like another continent.

That is the shape of a good three weeks in Australia. Not a list of everything, but a handful of places that feel completely different from one another: a harbour city, a sandstone wilderness, the red centre, a rainforest that runs down to a reef, and a cold southern coast. Here is how we would string them together.

## Why fewer stops makes a better trip

First-timers almost always underestimate the distances in Australia. Every region in this plan is a flight from the last, and each flight eats most of a day once you count the transfer, the check-in and the drive at the other end.

So we keep it to four regions in three weeks. That gives you at least three nights in each, which is the minimum for a place to stop feeling like a transit lounge. You get one slow morning everywhere, one day lost to weather without panic, and the chance to go back to the lookout or beach you liked best.

What we leave out, and why:

- **Perth and Western Australia.** Superb, but a long way west of everything else here. It deserves its own trip; see our [Western Australia coast guide](/stories/western-australia-coast-perth-to-exmouth/).
- **Kakadu and the Top End.** Wonderful in the dry season, but a fifth region would cost you rest days. Our [Kakadu story](/stories/kakadu-top-end-dry-season/) covers it.
- **Driving between regions.** The distances are the trip in themselves. Fly between regions and drive within them.

This version works best from about May to October, when the centre is cool enough to walk and the tropical north is in its dry season. Further down we explain what to swap if you are travelling in the southern summer.

## Week one: Sydney and the Blue Mountains

Give Sydney four nights. Spend the first day or two getting over the flight on foot: the harbour foreshore, a ferry to anywhere, a swim at a patrolled beach. Jet lag fades faster in daylight and salt water than in a hotel room.

Then go west into the mountains for two nights rather than a rushed day trip. [Blue Mountains National Park](https://www.nationalparks.nsw.gov.au/visit-a-park/parks/blue-mountains-national-park) is World Heritage-listed, and NSW National Parks points visitors to the Three Sisters from Echo Point in Katoomba, the view over the Grose Valley from Govetts Leap at Blackheath, and the lookouts and waterfalls around Wentworth Falls. The Grand Cliff Top Walk links Wentworth Falls, Leura and Katoomba if you want a proper day on your feet.

Staying up there means you see the cliffs at both ends of the day, when the light is low and the coach groups have gone. Base yourself in Katoomba or Blackheath.

The park is always open, but NSW National Parks notes it may close at times because of poor weather or fire danger, and some areas close overnight. A vehicle entry fee applies only at attractions in the Glenbrook area. Check the park page for alerts in the days before you go.

## Week two: the Red Centre, then the tropics

### Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa (three nights)

From Sydney you can fly direct to Yulara, the resort town beside the park; Parks Australia's [getting here page](https://uluru.gov.au/plan/getting-here/) lists direct flights from Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane and Cairns. That last one matters, because it lets you fly straight on to the reef afterwards without doubling back.

You need a [park pass](https://uluru.gov.au/plan/buy-your-pass/) to enter Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa National Park; buy it online before you arrive. Under-18s go free, and the pass covers this park only.

Three nights gives you two sunrises and two sunsets, which is what the place asks for. Walk the base of Uluṟu early, go to the Cultural Centre before you do anything else, and give a separate dawn to the domes of Kata Tjuṯa.

The [Uluṟu climb closed permanently](https://uluru.gov.au/discover/history/uluru-climb-closure/) on 26 October 2019. Climbing was not generally permitted under Tjukurpa, Aṉangu law, and Parks Australia says attempting it now breaches federal law. Follow the signs about sacred sites and photography too; they are there at Aṉangu request.

Parks Australia says [May to September is the best time to come](https://uluru.gov.au/plan/when-to-come/). From October to March it can get extremely hot, and the park advises not walking after 11 am in summer. Our full [Uluṟu and the Red Centre guide](/stories/uluru-red-centre-how-to-visit/) goes deeper.

### Port Douglas, the Daintree and the reef (four nights)

Fly from Yulara to Cairns and drive north to Port Douglas. The change hits you at the airport door: wet heat, the smell of cut cane and frangipani, green on every surface after the red.

Tourism Australia's [Daintree guide](https://www.australia.com/en/places/cairns-and-surrounds/guide-to-the-daintree-rainforest.html) explains that Daintree National Park has two parts: the Mossman Gorge area, close to Port Douglas, and the coastal Cape Tribulation section further north, reached by a car ferry over the Daintree River. The Kuku Yalanji are the Traditional Custodians, and an Aboriginal-guided walk from the Mossman Gorge Centre is the best way into the forest.

Spend one full day on the outer reef with a licensed operator, one day in the Daintree, and keep the rest loose for weather. If you have time for only one reef day, choose your operator carefully; our [Great Barrier Reef guide](/stories/great-barrier-reef-where-and-when/) explains how.

One rule for the whole tropical north: saltwater crocodiles live in rivers, swamps and sometimes beaches here. Surf Life Saving Australia's [crocodile advice](https://beachsafe.org.au/surf-safety/saltwater-crocodile) is plain: obey warning signs, and never enter water where crocodiles might live, even if there is no sign.

## Week three: Melbourne and the Great Ocean Road

Fly south from Cairns to Melbourne and you will probably want a jumper. Melbourne rewards three nights spent eating, wandering laneways and galleries, and catching a tram with no particular aim.

Then hire a car and take three nights on the Great Ocean Road. The coast west of Torquay runs through surf towns and the edge of [Great Otway National Park](https://www.parks.vic.gov.au/places-to-see/parks/great-otway-national-park), whose fern gullies hide waterfalls such as Triplet Falls and Hopetoun Falls, before it reaches the limestone stacks of the Twelve Apostles. Sleep a night in Lorne or Apollo Bay and another near Port Campbell so you are close to the stacks at the quiet ends of the day.

Our [Great Ocean Road driving guide](/stories/driving-the-great-ocean-road/) covers driving on the left, wildlife on the road at dusk, which direction to go, overnight stops and how to book the new Twelve Apostles visitor centre. Then fly home from Melbourne.

## The flights that hold it together

A rough sequence that avoids backtracking:

1. Arrive Sydney (four nights), then two nights in the Blue Mountains.
2. Sydney to Yulara (three nights).
3. Yulara to Cairns, then on to Port Douglas (four nights).
4. Cairns to Melbourne (three nights), then three nights on the Great Ocean Road.
5. Fly out of Melbourne.

That is three domestic flights, and it ends you in a different city from the one you arrive in. Book an open-jaw international ticket, into Sydney and out of Melbourne, so you are not flying back north at the end. Schedules into Yulara change, so check airlines before you lock in your dates. We would avoid any connection that needs you to arrive and leave a region on the same day.

## Season swaps: the wet season and a southern variant

Australia's seasons run opposite to the northern hemisphere's, and the tropics have their own calendar. Tourism Australia describes [a wet season from November to April](https://www.australia.com/en/facts-and-planning/when-to-go/australias-seasons.html) in Tropical North Queensland: very hot, humid, with monsoonal rain and possible cyclones. The same page notes it is stinger season on the reef, when snorkellers and divers need full-body wetsuits.

You can still go to Port Douglas in the wet. The rainforest is at its greenest, storms often come in the afternoon, and the reef can be calm. But if your dates land between December and March, consider a southern variant instead:

- **Keep Sydney and the Blue Mountains.** Summer is busy, so book early.
- **Think hard about the Red Centre.** Summer heat there is serious. Go only with early starts and an itinerary built around the 11 am cut-off for walking.
- **Swap the tropics for Tasmania.** Fly from Melbourne to Hobart, then drive to Freycinet and Cradle Mountain. Tasmania's cool summer is its best season. A [valid parks pass is required](https://parks.tas.gov.au/explore-our-parks/freycinet-national-park) for its national parks. See our [Tasmania in a week](/stories/tasmania-in-a-week/).
- **Do the Great Ocean Road in summer.** The evenings are long and the coast is at its best, although also at its busiest.

Park conditions, flight schedules and seasonal closures change from year to year. Check each linked official page in the weeks before you travel, and again a few days out. Our [seasons guide](/stories/australia-seasons-when-to-go/) helps if you are still choosing your month.
