Late afternoon on the Arnhem Highway, and the light has gone the colour of honey. Smoke from a patch burn drifts across the road, sweet and woody, and black kites wheel low over the blackened ground, waiting for whatever the fire flushes out. Termite mounds stand in the woodland like gravestones. Then the country opens up into floodplain, pale and wide and dotted with birds, and somewhere ahead the stone wall of the escarpment catches the last of the sun.

That is the Top End in the dry: warm, clear, smoky at the edges, and open for business. Come in the wet and the same country is green, roaring with rain and, in many places, cut off. This guide is about the dry season, roughly May to September or October, when most of Kakadu's sites are reachable, and about the parks either side of it, Litchfield and Nitmiluk, with Darwin as your base.

## Kakadu's six seasons, and why the dry matters

Most visitors think of northern Australia as having two seasons, wet and dry. Kakadu's Traditional Owners, Bininj/Mungguy, recognise six. Parks Australia's [guide to Kakadu's seasons](https://kakadu.gov.au/plan/when-come/seasons/) describes them, roughly:

- **Kudjewk**, the monsoon season, December to March: storms, heavy rain and flooding.
- **Bangkerreng**, April: the "knock 'em down" storms that flatten the speargrass, then clearing skies.
- **Yekke**, May to mid-June: cooler and less humid, with morning mists over the plains; time to start patch burning to clean the Country.
- **Wurrkeng**, mid-June to mid-August: the cold-weather season, by Territory standards. Low humidity, creeks stop flowing, and waterbirds crowd the shrinking billabongs.
- **Kurrung**, mid-August to mid-October: hot and dry.
- **Kunumeleng**, mid-October to late December: the pre-monsoon build-up, increasingly hot and humid, with afternoon storms.

The transitions are marked by the weather, by which plants are flowering and which bush foods are abundant, not by dates on a calendar. It is a richer way to read the place, and it also explains the practical picture. As water drains from the floodplains through Yekke and Wurrkeng, roads dry out and visitor sites reopen. Parks Australia says the [dry season is the most popular time to visit, with most visitor sites open](https://kakadu.gov.au/plan/when-to-come/), and recommends booking accommodation in advance because it gets busy.

For a first visit we'd aim for Yekke or Wurrkeng. By Kurrung the heat is climbing hard, and by the build-up you are trading comfort for crowds of wildlife at the last waterholes.

## Passes and seasonal openings

You need a [Kakadu park pass](https://kakadu.gov.au/plan/passes/), which you can buy online before your trip; the same page lists places to buy one if you can't, including the Bowali Visitor Centre near Jabiru, Cooinda Lodge and the Katherine Visitor Centre. Passes are valid for seven days, and the price varies with the season, so check the page rather than relying on an old figure. The Kakadu pass covers Kakadu only. Other Northern Territory parks need a separate pass (more on that below).

Kakadu opens site by site after the wet, not all at once. Parks Australia publishes a daily [Kakadu access report](https://kakadu.gov.au/news/access-and-alerts/) showing what is open, open with risks, or closed, along with target opening dates where it can give them. Make it a habit to check it every evening of your trip. Some closures are for safety, some for flood damage, and some at the request of Traditional Owners; all of them apply to you.

### Jim Jim and Twin Falls

These are the waterfalls on many people's wish lists, and the news for anyone travelling soon is not good. Parks Australia announced that the [Jim Jim and Twin Falls area would stay closed for the whole of 2026](https://kakadu.gov.au/news/jim-jim-and-twin-falls-area-remain-closed-2026/), including the falls, the Twin Falls gorge and escarpment walk, and Karnamarr campground. Cyclones badly damaged roads in the area, and Traditional Owners asked that it stay closed until road upgrades are complete. Parks Australia said it expected works to finish in time for the area to reopen for the 2027 dry season.

That is a plan, not a promise. If Jim Jim is central to your trip, check the [access report](https://kakadu.gov.au/news/access-and-alerts/) before you book anything. If it is shut, a scenic flight over the escarpment is the way to see this country from above.

## Rock art at Ubirr and Burrungkuy

Kakadu's rock art is the heart of any visit. Parks Australia names [Ubirr and Burrungkuy (Nourlangie) as the park's main rock art galleries](https://kakadu.gov.au/things-do/activities/rock-art/), where you can see naturalistic paintings of animals, x-ray art and paintings recording early contact with Europeans. The local word for rock art is kunbim.

What changes how you look at it is the park's explanation that, for Bininj/Mungguy, the act of painting has generally mattered more than the painting itself, which is why older images are often painted over by newer ones. These are not relics. The art shows tools still used, animals still hunted and stories still told, now more often on bark, paper and canvas.

Some practical notes:

- **Ubirr** sits in the north-east of the park near the East Alligator River. Go in the late afternoon, walk the art sites, then climb to the lookout over the floodplain for the evening light. It is busy at sunset, and worth it.
- **Burrungkuy** is closer to the middle of the park and is one of the areas Parks Australia says [stays open all year](https://kakadu.gov.au/plan/when-to-come/). Go early, before the heat builds in the rock shelters.
- Stay on the paths and boardwalks, and don't touch the paintings.

## Yellow Water and the wetlands

The other essential is a boat on Yellow Water, the billabong near Cooinda in the centre of the park. In the dry the floodplain shrinks back to permanent water, and the birds and crocodiles come with it: magpie geese, eagles and crowds of other waterbirds, and large saltwater crocodiles watching from the bank. Yellow Water is another area that is [open all year](https://kakadu.gov.au/plan/when-to-come/), and cruises run on the billabong; we'd book the first departure of the day for the softest light and coolest air. Prices and timetables change, so check with the operator directly.

## Crocodiles and the water

This is the one section to read twice. Saltwater crocodiles live throughout the Top End, in rivers, billabongs, estuaries, the sea and plenty of places that look like inviting swimming holes. The Northern Territory's [safety advice](https://northernterritory.com/plan/useful-information/safety-information) says plainly that any body of water in the Top End may contain large and potentially dangerous crocodiles, and asks you to observe safety signs and Be Crocwise.

In Kakadu, Parks Australia's [Staying safe page](https://kakadu.gov.au/plan/plan-your-trip/staying-safe/) adds that crocodiles usually hunt by staying submerged and can attack in knee-deep water, so even wading is dangerous, and that the safest place to swim in Kakadu is a public or hotel pool. Our rules are simple:

- Never swim unless a sign specifically says the spot is safe for swimming, and if in doubt, don't.
- Keep well back from the water's edge, especially at boat ramps and river crossings.
- Don't camp or linger right at the water's edge.
- Swimming spots open and close as conditions change. The [access report](https://kakadu.gov.au/news/access-and-alerts/) recently listed Maguk as closed for crocodile management, which tells you how seriously the rangers take this.

## Heat, hydration and remoteness

Even in the "cool" months, Top End days are hot. Kakadu's [safety guidance](https://kakadu.gov.au/plan/plan-your-trip/staying-safe/) recommends drinking at least four litres of water a day, wearing a broad-brimmed hat, long loose clothing and high-factor sunscreen, and walking in the cooler parts of the day. Start walks at first light, rest through the middle of the day, and head out again late in the afternoon.

The same page warns that most of the park has no mobile phone coverage, so tell someone where you are going and when you expect to be back. Carry more water than you think you need, and a first aid kit.

## Litchfield and Nitmiluk

Kakadu is the headline, but the parks either side of it are reasons to stay longer.

**Litchfield National Park** is the easy one: roughly an hour or so south of Darwin by road, with waterfalls, monsoon forest, walking tracks and fields of huge termite mounds. It works as a long day trip from Darwin or an overnight stop. Some of its waterholes are popular swimming spots in the dry, but the crocodile rule above still applies: swim only where signs say it is safe, and check the latest status before you go.

**Nitmiluk National Park**, just outside Katherine, a few hours' drive south of Darwin, is Jawoyn Country. The gorge system is the draw, and you can see it by boat, by canoe or on foot along the rim. Katherine also makes a sensible link if you are driving between Kakadu and the south.

Both are Northern Territory Government parks, not Parks Australia ones, so your Kakadu pass doesn't cover them. According to [Tourism NT's passes and permits page](https://northernterritory.com/plan/useful-information/passes-and-permits), visitors from interstate and overseas need an NT parks entry pass for most other parks and reserves in the Territory, and if you're on a guided tour it may already be included. The same page explains that travelling onto Aboriginal land, such as Arnhem Land across the East Alligator River from Kakadu, generally needs a permit from the relevant land council. Rules change, so check before you go.

## Darwin as a base, and the roads

Darwin is where nearly every Top End trip starts. It has the international and domestic flights, the hire cars, the tour departures and the supermarkets for stocking up. From there, Kakadu is roughly three hours' drive east, and a loop through Kakadu, Katherine and Litchfield is a classic week or more on the road. If you prefer not to drive, plenty of tours run from the city.

If you are driving, check the [Road Report NT](https://roadreport.nt.gov.au/home) every day, and the Kakadu access report for roads inside the park. Kakadu's [safety page](https://kakadu.gov.au/plan/plan-your-trip/staying-safe/) notes that some roads in and out of the park are 4WD only, with steep, narrow sections that turn slippery when wet. Read your hire agreement before you plan any unsealed detours, because rental companies may restrict where you can take the car. Fill up with fuel whenever you have the chance, and avoid driving at dusk and after dark, when wildlife and wandering cattle are hardest to see.
