The ferry shudders as it pulls away from Circular Quay. Behind you the city is waking up: the smell of coffee and diesel, gulls working the wharf, light coming off the water so hard you squint even in sunglasses. Ahead, the harbour opens out in a sprawl of sandstone headlands, bush-covered points and little beaches, and it dawns on you that this is just one city on one stretch of one coast.

That is the first thing to understand about Australia. It is very big, and almost everything else about planning a good first trip follows from that. This guide covers the basics we would want a friend to know before booking: how to think about distance, what you need before you fly, how to stay safe in the sun and the sea, and how to be a good guest on Country.

## Think in regions, not the whole map

Most of the places first-timers dream about are a long way from each other. Sydney, Melbourne, Uluṟu and the Great Barrier Reef are not a loop you drive in a week; between them sit flights of several hours and, in some cases, a change of plane.

The trips we see go wrong are the ones that try to see everything. You spend half your holiday in airports and arrive everywhere too tired to enjoy it. The trips that work pick three or four regions and give each one room to breathe.

A sensible first-timer's shape might be:

- **Sydney** for the harbour, the beaches and a day or two in the Blue Mountains.
- **Melbourne** for food, laneways and perhaps the start of the [Great Ocean Road](/stories/driving-the-great-ocean-road/).
- **The Red Centre** for Uluṟu and Kata Tjuṯa (our guide to [visiting Uluru and the Red Centre well](/stories/uluru-red-centre-how-to-visit/) goes deeper).
- **The Great Barrier Reef** from Cairns or Port Douglas (see [where to go and when](/stories/great-barrier-reef-where-and-when/)).

If you have three weeks, our [3-week Australia itinerary](/stories/three-week-australia-itinerary/) shows one way to pace it. If you have ten days, drop a region rather than squeezing.

## Before you fly: visas and what you can bring

Sort out your visa or travel authority before you fly, even for a short holiday. Which one you need depends on your passport.

The [Electronic Travel Authority (subclass 601)](https://immi.homeaffairs.gov.au/visas/getting-a-visa/visa-listing/electronic-travel-authority-601) is for holders of ETA-eligible passports travelling as tourists, to visit family or friends, or as business visitors. You apply through the Australian ETA app, you must be outside Australia when you apply, and each visit can be up to three months at a time. The same Home Affairs page notes that European passport holders may be eligible for an eVisitor (subclass 651) instead, and that other passport holders may need a Visitor (subclass 600) visa. Home Affairs suggests you don't make travel arrangements until your ETA is granted, so sort it out before you book anything you can't change.

Visa rules and eligible passports change. Check your own eligibility on the Home Affairs site rather than relying on a friend's experience or an old forum post, and use only the official channels it lists.

Then there is biosecurity. Australia takes what crosses its border seriously, and the arrivals hall is where you will notice it. The Australian Border Force's [Can you bring it in?](https://www.abf.gov.au/entering-and-leaving-australia/can-you-bring-it-in) guide lists fresh fruit and homemade food as things not to bring, and prescription medicines as something to declare. Our advice is simple: eat or bin the plane snacks before you land, keep medicines in their original packaging, and when you are unsure about anything in your bag, declare it.

## When to go, briefly

Seasons in the south are the reverse of the Northern Hemisphere: summer around Christmas, winter in the middle of the year. The tropical north works differently again, with a wet season and a dry season rather than four neat seasons, and the centre swings between fierce summer heat and cold desert nights in winter.

The short version is that there is no single best month for the whole country, only a best month for each region. We have unpacked it region by region in [Australia's seasons: when to go where](/stories/australia-seasons-when-to-go/).

## Getting around: flights, roads and the left-hand side

For the big jumps, fly. Domestic flights link the capitals and the main regional gateways, and they turn what would be days of driving into a morning. Save driving for regions where the road is the experience: the Great Ocean Road, Tasmania, the coast of Western Australia.

When you do drive, the headline is in Tourism Australia's [driving guide for first-timers](https://www.australia.com/en/facts-and-planning/getting-around/driving-in-australia.html): Australians drive on the left, give way to the right at roundabouts and intersections, wear seatbelts, and must not use a hand-held phone while driving, even stopped at lights. The same guide says you can drive on a foreign licence for up to three months if it is in English, and that otherwise you will need an International Driving Permit. Rules like this can vary and change, so confirm with your hire company and the road authority of the state you're driving in.

A few things to check before you sign a hire agreement:

- Which licences and permits the company accepts.
- Whether the vehicle is allowed on unsealed roads, and what happens to your insurance if you leave the bitumen.
- Whether you can drop the car in a different city.
- How tolls are handled on city motorways.

Above all, respect the distances. Out of the cities, towns and fuel stops can be far apart and phone coverage patchy. Tourism Australia's guide advises not driving at night in outback and country areas because of animals on the road, and we would underline that. Plan your days so you arrive before dusk.

## Sun, surf and heat

The Australian sun is stronger than many visitors expect. Cancer Council Australia's [UV Index page](https://www.cancer.org.au/cancer-information/causes-and-prevention/sun-safety/uv-index) recommends following the five SunSmart steps whenever the UV is 3 or above, and points out that UV can be high even on cool and overcast days. The steps are easy to remember: slip on covering clothing, slop on sunscreen, slap on a hat, seek shade and slide on sunglasses.

At the beach, the rule is to swim between the red and yellow flags. Surf Life Saving Australia's [flags and signs guide](https://beachsafe.org.au/surf-safety/flags-and-signs) explains that those flags mark the supervised area where a lifesaving service is operating, and says that if there are no red and yellow flags, check with lifeguards and if unsure, don't go in. Many beautiful beaches are unpatrolled. Admire them from the sand.

In the tropical north, the sea brings its own hazards. Beachsafe's [tropical stingers page](https://beachsafe.org.au/surf-safety/tropical-stingers) advises swimming at patrolled beaches inside stinger nets where provided, and wearing a full-body lycra suit, particularly during stinger season, which it describes as generally November to March. It also explains that vinegar is the first-aid treatment for tropical stings, alongside calling Triple Zero (000).

Then there are crocodiles. Beachsafe's [saltwater crocodile guidance](https://beachsafe.org.au/surf-safety/saltwater-crocodiles) is blunt: obey warning signs, and never enter the water where crocodiles might live, even if there is no sign. In the Top End and far north Queensland, that rules out a lot of inviting-looking water.

Heat is the last piece. In the centre and the north, walk early, rest in the middle of the day, and carry more water than you think you will need.

## Wildlife and the outdoors

Seeing a kangaroo, wombat or echidna in the wild is a highlight for many first-timers, and it is easy to do well. Keep your distance and don't feed anything. Tasmania's Parks and Wildlife Service, in its [caring for wildlife guidance](https://parks.tas.gov.au/discovery-and-learning/wildlife/caring-for-wildlife), asks visitors to keep at least two metres from native animals and resist the temptation to feed them, and explains how feeding harms animals and makes some of them bold enough to injure people.

Beyond that, the usual outdoor courtesies apply: stay on marked tracks, take your rubbish with you, and check park alerts before you set out. Many national parks need a park pass, so look that up on the park's official site when you plan. For where to find animals without a fence between you, read [where to see Australian wildlife in the wild](/stories/where-to-see-australian-wildlife/).

## Country and respect

Everywhere you travel in Australia is Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander Country, with its own Traditional Owners, languages and law. Being a good visitor is mostly about listening and following what you are asked to do.

The clearest example is Uluṟu. Parks Australia explains on its [Uluṟu climb closure page](https://uluru.gov.au/discover/history/uluru-climb-closure/) that climbing was not generally permitted under Tjukurpa, Aṉangu law and Culture, that the climb closed on 26 October 2019, and that climbing now breaches federal environment law and attracts penalties. The park also asks visitors not to photograph certain culturally sensitive sites, which are signposted.

The same spirit applies across the country. Use the place names the Traditional Owners and park authorities use, take the cultural tours run by Aboriginal guides where you can, ask before photographing people, and treat signs asking you to stay out of an area as you would a closed door in someone's home.

## Money, time zones and the small things

We won't give you a tipping formula, because there isn't an official one we can point to. You will see local practice quickly enough. Make sure you have a card that works overseas, and tell your bank you are travelling.

Time zones catch out plenty of travellers moving between regions. According to [Tourism Australia's time zone guide](https://www.australia.com/en/facts-and-planning/about-australia/time-zones.html), there are three standard zones: Eastern (UTC +10), Central (UTC +9.5) and Western (UTC +8). New South Wales, Victoria, Tasmania, the ACT and South Australia move to daylight saving time from the first Sunday in October to the first Sunday in April, while Queensland, the Northern Territory and Western Australia do not. In practice, that means Sydney and Brisbane, or Adelaide and Darwin, are an hour apart in summer but not in winter, so check flight arrival times carefully.

Finally, go easy on yourself in the first couple of days. The long-haul flight, the light and the jet lag all take a toll. Book something low-key for your first day, walk the foreshore, and let the scale of the place sink in before you start crossing it.
