The first thing you notice is the air. Step out of the terminal in Hobart and it is cool and clean, with a faint edge of eucalyptus and salt from the Derwent. By late afternoon the light goes long and gold across the sandstone warehouses of Salamanca Place, and behind the city the bulk of kunanyi / Mount Wellington wears a cap of cloud that may or may not lift before dinner. That uncertainty is Tasmania in miniature: an island where the weather writes half the itinerary and you write the rest.

A week is enough to make a proper loop: the south, the east coast, the north and the high country, then back down the middle. It is not enough to see everything, and we would rather you did fewer things well. Here is how we would spend it.

## How the loop works

The shape is simple. Start in Hobart, take a day trip down to Port Arthur, then drive up the east coast to Freycinet. Cross to Launceston, head west into the mountains at Cradle, and return to Hobart through the Midlands. If you fly into Hobart and out of Launceston you can skip the last leg and buy yourself an extra half-day somewhere you love.

Distances look small on a map, and compared with the mainland they are. But Tasmanian roads are narrow, hilly and full of bends, and the drives take longer than the kilometres suggest. Plan on a few hours of driving on most travel days, and do not stack long drives back to back.

A rough shape for seven days:

- **Days 1–2:** Hobart, Salamanca and kunanyi / Mount Wellington
- **Day 3:** Port Arthur and the Tasman Peninsula
- **Days 4–5:** Up the east coast to Freycinet and Wineglass Bay
- **Day 6:** Launceston, then on to Cradle Mountain
- **Day 7:** Cradle Mountain at first light, then back via the Midlands

## Hobart, Salamanca and the mountain

Give Hobart two nights. The waterfront is compact enough to walk, and Salamanca Place is where most visitors find their feet: galleries, cafés and pubs in old stone warehouses, and on Saturdays a busy market that spills along the street. Wander up the steps into Battery Point for the cottages and the harbour views, and eat well. Hobart takes its food seriously.

Then go up the mountain. kunanyi / Mount Wellington sits inside Wellington Park, which the [Wellington Park Management Trust](https://www.wellingtonpark.org.au/) says is free to enter and open every day. On a clear morning the summit view runs out over the city, the river and a long way south. On a bad one you will see your own breath and not much else, so check the forecast and dress for winter whatever the month. Pinnacle Road to the summit closes from time to time for weather, events and maintenance, and the Trust posts closures on its homepage, so look before you set off.

If you have a spare half-day, Bruny Island is the classic add-on. Discover Tasmania notes that you [reach Bruny by vehicle ferry from Kettering](https://www.discovertasmania.com.au/regions/hobart-and-south/bruny-island/), a short drive south of Hobart. It is worth a full day if you can steal one from later in the week: cliffs, beaches and local produce, and a slower pace than the mainland of the island.

## Port Arthur and the Tasman Peninsula

Port Arthur is a sobering, beautiful place, and it deserves most of a day. The [Port Arthur Historic Site](https://portarthur.org.au/), run by the Port Arthur Historic Site Management Authority, is one of the 11 places that together make up the UNESCO-listed Australian Convict Sites World Heritage Property. The site's own website puts it at about a 90-minute drive from Hobart.

The ruined penitentiary and the green lawns sloping to the water can feel almost peaceful on a still morning, which makes the stories you learn there land harder. A general admission ticket lets you explore at your own pace. Guided tours, evening ghost tours and a ferry trip to the Isle of the Dead are also on offer. Check the official site for current hours, tours and ticket options before you go.

On the drive down and back, the Tasman Peninsula's sea cliffs and blowholes make good leg-stretching stops. Leave yourself time; this is not a road to rush.

## The east coast and Freycinet

From Hobart the road north to the east coast passes farmland, then the sea comes into view and stays there. Swansea and the villages around Great Oyster Bay are good places to stop for lunch, and by the time you reach Coles Bay the pink granite peaks of the Hazards are filling the windscreen.

[Freycinet National Park](https://parks.tas.gov.au/explore-our-parks/freycinet-national-park) is best known for Wineglass Bay, and the walk up to the saddle lookout is the one almost everyone does. Go early. The light is softer, the car park quieter and the climb cooler. If you have the legs, continue down to the beach itself; it is a different experience at sand level. Other options include shorter strolls, kayaking from Coles Bay and the Cape Tourville lookout, where Parks and Wildlife Service notes you may see sea-eagles and gannets.

Two practical points. First, Parks and Wildlife Service warns that drinking water is scarce in the park, so fill your bottles before you set out. Second, you need a parks pass here (more on that below), and the visitor centre sells them.

Two nights here lets you walk one day and kayak or simply sit on a beach the next. If your week is tight, one night will do, but you will want more.

## Launceston and the road to Cradle

Launceston is a gentler, greener city than Hobart, set where the Tamar meets the South Esk. Cataract Gorge is right on the edge of town and an easy walk. If you like wine, the Tamar Valley vineyards are close. Many people treat Launceston as a stopover, but it is pleasant for a night in its own right.

From there the road climbs west into the high country. Cradle Mountain sits in the north of [Cradle Mountain-Lake St Clair National Park](https://parks.tas.gov.au/explore-our-parks/cradle-mountain), which Parks and Wildlife Service notes became part of the Tasmanian Wilderness World Heritage Area in 1982. Arrive by mid-afternoon if you can. The wildlife comes out as the light fades, and you do not want to be driving the last stretch in the dark.

## Cradle Mountain and the drive home

This is the high point of the week, in every sense. On a calm morning Dove Lake mirrors the jagged dolerite peak, and the Dove Lake Circuit takes you along the shore and through the Ballroom Forest, a pocket of cool temperate rainforest. If the mountain is hidden in cloud, it is still a fine walk, and the cloud can lift in minutes.

You will not drive yourself to the lake for most of the day. Parks and Wildlife Service says that during operating hours visitors should [park at the visitor centre and take the shuttle bus](https://parks.tas.gov.au/explore-our-parks/cradle-mountain/cradle-mountain-shuttle-bus-information), which runs along the narrow, winding road to Dove Lake with stops along the way. You need a valid parks pass, and the shuttle needs its own ticket unless your pass covers it. Shuttle times change with the season and the weather, so check with the visitor centre on arrival.

Here more than anywhere, take the weather seriously. Parks and Wildlife Service is blunt about it: in Tasmania's mountains [snow, hail, rain, wind and sun are all possible at any time of the year](https://parks.tas.gov.au/safety-in-parks), and under-equipped walkers have died. Even for a short walk, carry a waterproof jacket, a warm layer, a hat and water. For anything longer, read the full safety guidance and register your walk.

The drive back to Hobart runs through the Midlands, a dry, open landscape of sheep country, old stone bridges and Georgian villages. It is the most relaxed driving of the week. Stop for coffee, stretch your legs, and let the trip settle.

## Parks passes, roads and wildlife

**Parks passes.** [A valid parks pass is required for entry to all of Tasmania's national parks](https://parks.tas.gov.au/explore-our-parks/know-before-you-go/park-passes-and-entry-fees), and there are several types. On this route you need one for Freycinet and Cradle Mountain at the very least. Shuttles, campgrounds and some walks have separate fees. Pass options and prices change, so buy from the official page and read the details there.

**Roads.** Expect narrow roads, tight corners and steep sections, especially in the north-west and on the way to Cradle. Slow down, use the pull-over bays to let locals pass, and do not plan to cover long distances after dark.

**Wildlife on the roads.** This is the one that catches visitors out. Parks and Wildlife Service notes that native animals are [especially active early and late in the day](https://parks.tas.gov.au/discovery-and-learning/wildlife/caring-for-wildlife), that Tasmanian devils are nocturnal and hard to see at night, and it urges drivers to slow down between dusk and dawn. Wallabies, pademelons and wombats often wander onto the road. Drive in daylight where you can. If you must drive at dusk, drive slowly. Only stop to help an injured animal if it is safe to do so.

**Weather.** It can change within the hour, and a sunny morning at sea level is no guide to the plateau. Pack layers whatever the month.

## When to go

Summer, roughly December to March, is the main season: long days, warmer water at Freycinet, and the best odds of clear mornings at Cradle. It is also the busiest time, so book accommodation early, particularly on the east coast and at Cradle. Parks and Wildlife Service notes that bushfires can occur between October and March. Check the park alerts and fire bans before you go, because closures can happen at short notice.

Autumn brings settled weather and good colour, and it is often our favourite time. Spring is fresh and green, with longer days arriving. Winter is cold, dark early and often snowy in the highlands, but quiet, cosy and good for food. If you go in winter, allow more time on the roads and check conditions every day.

Whatever the season, check the official pages linked above before you travel. Park access, shuttle times and road conditions all change, sometimes overnight.
