Somewhere past Lorne the road stops pretending to be a highway. It narrows, tucks itself against the cliff and starts to swing hard left and right above a sea that is green close in and slate-grey further out. Wind pushes at the car on the exposed bends. In the gaps between headlands you get a few seconds of white water, a surfer sitting out beyond the break, a gull hanging still in the updraught. Then the next bend, and the next.

The Great Ocean Road is one of the most famous drives in Australia, and it deserves the reputation. It also punishes people who try to do it in a day from Melbourne. Here is how we would drive it.

## The route, from Torquay to the Shipwreck Coast

Tourism Australia describes Torquay, [just over an hour's drive from Melbourne](https://www.australia.com/en/places/melbourne-and-surrounds/guide-to-the-great-ocean-road.html), as the road's official starting point. From there it runs west along the coast of south-west Victoria. The main stops, in the order you meet them heading west:

- **Torquay and Anglesea.** Surf beaches, and the first glimpse of the cliffs.
- **Aireys Inlet and Lorne.** Lorne is a proper town with cafés and a long beach, and a natural first-night stop.
- **Kennett River and Apollo Bay.** The road is at its most dramatic between Lorne and Apollo Bay.
- **The Otways.** Inland of Apollo Bay the road climbs through tall forest before dropping back to the coast.
- **Port Campbell National Park.** The Twelve Apostles, Loch Ard Gorge, Gibson Steps and the town of Port Campbell.
- **Beyond.** The coast continues towards Warrnambool and the stacks of the Bay of Islands.

The land belongs to the Wadawurrung and Eastern Maar peoples, whom the Great Ocean Road Coast and Parks Authority acknowledges as Traditional Owners of the road's land and waters.

## How many days, and which direction

Two nights is the minimum we would plan, and three is better. Done in a day, the road becomes a long stretch of bends with a crowded car park at the end of it. With two nights, you can stop whenever something catches your eye.

A simple two-night plan:

1. **Day one:** Melbourne to Lorne or Apollo Bay, with beach stops along the way. Sleep in either town.
2. **Day two:** Through the Otways to the Twelve Apostles. Sleep in or near Port Campbell.
3. **Day three:** Early start at the stacks and Loch Ard Gorge, then back to Melbourne on the inland route, which is quicker than retracing the coast.

Add a third night in Apollo Bay or the Otways if you want time for waterfalls and a rainforest walk.

**Which direction?** We drive it east to west, from Torquay. Because Australians drive on the left, heading west puts you in the lane nearest the sea, which makes it easier to pull into the ocean-side lookouts. It also means you reach the Apostles at the end of the day. Going west to east works too: you start with the big set piece and finish with the surf towns. Either way, choose a direction and don't drive the whole coast both ways.

## Driving on the left, wildlife and fatigue

If you have never driven on the left, this road is a demanding place to learn. Tourism Australia's [driving guide](https://www.australia.com/en/facts-and-planning/getting-around/driving-in-australia.html) explains the basics: keep left and overtake on the right; give way to the right at roundabouts. It also suggests hiring an automatic if left-side driving is new to you. We would add a few things:

- **Use the pull-outs.** If a queue builds behind you, let it pass. Nobody minds, and it takes the pressure off.
- **Never stop on a bend for a photo.** Drive on to the next signed lookout.
- **Avoid driving at dusk and after dark.** Tourism Australia advises against night driving in country areas because animals such as kangaroos are very hard to see in the dark. On the Otways stretch, plan to be at your overnight stop before the light goes.
- **Take fatigue seriously.** Long-haul flights and narrow, winding roads are a bad combination. Transport for NSW's [fatigue advice](https://www.transport.nsw.gov.au/roadsafety/topics-tips/fatigue) applies everywhere: take regular breaks, share the driving, and pull over for a 20-minute nap if you feel drowsy. Don't drive the coast on your arrival day.

Landslips, roadworks and bushfires can close sections of the road. Check [VicTraffic](https://traffic.transport.vic.gov.au/), the Victorian Government's disruptions map, on the morning you set out.

## Koalas, waterfalls and the Otways

[Great Otway National Park](https://www.parks.vic.gov.au/places-to-see/parks/great-otway-national-park) runs from Torquay along the coast and up into the Otways hinterland. Parks Victoria and the Great Ocean Road Coast and Parks Authority manage different parts of it. The inland fern gullies hide waterfalls such as Triplet Falls and Hopetoun Falls, and Parks Victoria also lists a glow-worm walk at Kennett River, worth it if you are staying close by. Some walks and falls close from time to time, so check the park page before you detour.

[Kennett River](https://www.greatoceanroadauthority.vic.gov.au/Land-We-Manage/Coastal-Reserves/Kennett-River), halfway between Lorne and Apollo Bay, is where the Great Ocean Road Coast and Parks Authority says you can see koalas feeding in the trees of the national park. Look up into the eucalypts, keep your voice down, and watch from a distance. Don't feed any wildlife, and park only where it is signed. The village gets busy, so watch for people stepping onto the road while gazing up into the trees.

## The Twelve Apostles, Loch Ard Gorge and Port Campbell

[Port Campbell National Park](https://www.greatoceanroadauthority.vic.gov.au/Land-We-Manage/National-Parks/Port-Campbell-National-Park) is where the Southern Ocean has carved the limestone into cliffs, stacks, arches and caves. As well as the Twelve Apostles, the park's authority points visitors to Loch Ard Gorge, London Bridge, The Grotto, The Arch and Gibson Steps. It also notes that little penguins can be seen returning from the sea at dusk near the Twelve Apostles and London Bridge.

**Book the Twelve Apostles before you go.** The viewing area is now reached through the new Keerray Woorroong Visitor Centre, and the official site says [bookings are essential](https://www.twelveapostles.org.au/plan-your-visit/). It sits on the Great Ocean Road about 7 km east of Port Campbell. Book your slot when you know your dates, and check the opening and car-park hours on the same page.

**Loch Ard Gorge beach access** was closed because of rockfall risk and faults in the staircase. The authority has [described repair work](https://www.twelveapostles.org.au/returning-beach-access-at-loch-ard-gorge-safely/) to reopen it. The lookouts above the gorge are the reason to come anyway, but check whether the stairs are open before you promise anyone a walk on the sand.

Sleep in Port Campbell itself. The park has limited facilities, while the town has accommodation, places to eat and a short drive to the stacks for first light, before the day traffic arrives.

## Beach safety on a wild coast

This is not a gentle coast. The sea along the Great Ocean Road can be cold, powerful and full of rips, and the most beautiful beaches are often the most dangerous.

Surf Life Saving Australia calls [rip currents](https://beachsafe.org.au/surf-safety/ripcurrents) the number one hazard on Australian beaches, and its advice is simple:

- **Swim only at a patrolled beach, between the red and yellow flags.** Kennett River's beach, for example, is patrolled in the peak season. Many beaches along the road are not patrolled at all.
- **Learn to spot a rip.** Look for deeper or darker water, fewer breaking waves, sandy water heading out past the surf, or drifting seaweed.
- **If you are caught, stay calm.** Raise an arm and call for help, float with the current, or swim parallel to the beach.

At the stacks and gorges, stay behind fences and off cliff edges. These cliffs are still eroding, and the authority's own work at Loch Ard Gorge shows how real the rockfall risk is.

## When to go and where to stay

Tourism Australia names summer, December to February, as [the best time to drive](https://www.australia.com/en/places/melbourne-and-surrounds/guide-to-the-great-ocean-road.html), with clear skies, and winter, June to August, as the low season. Summer also brings the crowds. Book your Lorne or Apollo Bay bed and your Twelve Apostles slot early, and avoid weekends and school holidays if you can.

Autumn and spring are our favourite compromise: quieter towns, fine driving days and some big seas. Winter is cold, wet and moody, with short days. That cuts into your safe driving time, but the coast looks magnificent in a storm. Pack a warm jacket in every season; the weather here changes fast.

For overnight stops, think in pairs: Lorne or Apollo Bay for the first night, Port Campbell for the second. If you want to slow down further, add a night in the Otways. If you are combining the drive with the rest of the country, our [three-week Australia itinerary](/stories/three-week-australia-itinerary/) shows how it fits.

Road conditions, bookings and beach access all change, sometimes from week to week. Check VicTraffic and the official park pages before you set off.
